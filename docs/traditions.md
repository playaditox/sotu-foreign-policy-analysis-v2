# The four traditions in context

The four traditions describe overlapping reasons offered for foreign policy. They are interpretive categories, not permanent presidential identities or measures of hawkishness. The report’s 32 paired contextual examples illustrate these distinctions; their selection does not estimate prevalence. The diagnostic v2 curves and administration bars are not validated tradition-prevalence estimates.

## Wilsonian

Make peace and legitimacy more durable through rights, representative government and shared rules.

Human rights; democratic self-government; legal equality; international institutions with a stated normative purpose.

Boundary: A rights-based justification can support intervention. Firmness or a mention of the UN alone does not establish the rationale.

## Hamiltonian

Sustain prosperity and strategic position through commerce, credible commitments and a workable international order.

Market access; economic interdependence; commercial security; balance of power; strategic alliance credibility.

Boundary: Generic national interest needs a recoverable mechanism. Limiting commitments for strategic effectiveness differs from opposing external commitments on principle.

## Jacksonian

Prioritize and defend the American community, sovereign choice, citizens and national standing.

Protecting Americans; conditional obligations; resisting coercion; reciprocal burden-sharing; decisive response to a direct threat.

Boundary: Selective engagement can be forceful. Patriotism, jobs, tariffs or military vocabulary alone do not establish this rationale.

## Jeffersonian

Limit external exposure to protect the domestic republic from indefinite commitments, costs and threats to liberty.

Avoiding entanglement; finite missions; overextension; intervention costs; domestic opportunity costs; reasoned limits on force.

Boundary: Restraint needs an endorsed limit and a reason. Domestic spending, measured diplomacy or a tactical redeployment alone is insufficient.
